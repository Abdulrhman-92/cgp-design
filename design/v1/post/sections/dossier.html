The brief arrived the way the best ones do — a sketch on a napkin, a circle with two lines through it, and one sentence: “I want to see the water.” No budget talk, no part list. Just a client who had decided a computer could be a kinetic sculpture, and a workshop that had decided to agree.

§01 The Brief

The Hotwheel was commissioned as a render box first and a showpiece second — which changed everything about how we approached it. A display piece can run warm and quiet-hide its sins behind fan curves. A machine that renders eight hours a day cannot. So the wheel shape had to earn its keep thermally, not just visually, and every aesthetic decision had to survive a sustained-load argument.

The chassis would tilt at 45 degrees, the loops would run visible along the rim, and the whole assembly would hide nothing and apologize for nothing. From the shop floor, the napkin sketch got pinned above the bending bench — where it stayed for the eleven weeks that followed.

§02 Why Two Loops

The second loop is this entry’s real subject. A single loop through a CPU and a GPU is simpler, cheaper, and utterly a compromise: one coolant temperature means one component always runs warmer than it should. We split the circuits instead — LOOP_01 tuned silent for the CPU, LOOP_02 tuned aggressive for the GPU — each with its own pump, its own radiator bank, its own fill port.

The bonus nobody asks for until they need it: maintenance. Drain one loop and the other keeps the machine alive. During the Hotwheel’s first service, the CPU loop was swapped, flushed, and refilled while a render ran — uninterrupted — on the GPU loop.

“A single loop is a compromise. Two loops is a decision.”

— Forge floor rule #3

§03 Mapping The Wheel

Fourteen bends make the wheel, and every one of them was rehearsed. We printed a full-scale routing jig — the same discipline we bring to the compact builds — and bent each run against it before the tube ever saw the chassis. The rim radius stays constant for the entire circuit; the eye reads it as a drawn circle rather than plumbing, and that is the whole trick.

Eleven runs failed the jig. They sit in a drawer in the workshop, labeled and kept — a lesson in humility with a wall of successful builds above it.

§04 The Distro Ring

A circular chassis asks an uncomfortable question: where does the distribution plate go? The answer took three prototypes — a ring. CNC-cut acrylic, twin O-ring grooves, and channels that feed both loops from a single visible annulus at the hub. It is the part people photograph first and the part we machined last, after two failed glue-ups taught us where the leak paths really live.

§05 The 48-Hour Baptism

Every CGP machine serves a 48-hour leak test before it ships. The Hotwheel’s first attempt died at hour thirty-one — a hairline fracture at bend eleven, weeping one drop every twenty minutes onto a paper towel nobody wanted to see. The run was scrapped, the bend re-made against the jig, and the clock restarted from zero. The second baptism closed at forty-eight hours with a pressure log flatter than the Riyadh horizon.
